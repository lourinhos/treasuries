
# Can Lagged Yield Changes Beat a Random Walk?

## Group
Jonah Leo Malcolm, Jonah Leo Malcolm

## Changes since the proposal
We replaced a single train/test split with eight expanding-window, quarterly validation folds to tune the models without shuffling observations. We also added a paired block-bootstrap confidence interval for the comparison against the zero-change forecast as requested in the proposal feedback.

## Data
U.S. Treasury daily par yield curve, 2020–2023: 1,001 trading-day observations (251 in 2020, 251 in 2021, 249 in 2022, 250 in 2023). The target is the next daily change in the 10-year yield, measured in basis points. The selected linear-regression model uses one input: the preceding day's yield change. Two initial rows are lost when constructing the first difference and its lag. No 10-year yields are missing.

## Split
Eight chronological folds validate on each successive quarter of 2021–2022, training on all preceding observations from 2020 onward. No folds are shuffled. After selecting the lag length, we refit on 2020–2022 and score on the held-out 2023 observations (n=250). The 2023 outcomes are excluded from tuning. Models and folds are deterministic and the bootstrap uses seed 5420.

## Baseline
Ordinary least-squares linear regression, with the number of lagged daily changes p tuned over {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}. Eight-fold expanding-window validation selects p=1 (mean quarterly RMSE 6.052 bp; standard deviation across quarters 1.963 bp). The comparison forecast is zero daily change.

## Result
In 2023, linear regression has RMSE 7.486 bp, versus 7.461 bp for the zero-change forecast. The paired RMSE difference (regression minus zero) is +0.025 bp; its 95% confidence interval is [−0.018, 0.066] bp. The interval comes from 10,000 paired circular moving-block bootstrap resamples of 20 trading days. It includes zero, so the result does not establish an improvement over the random walk.

## Issues found
Not any noticeable ones. Treatment of this dataset is fairly standardized.
